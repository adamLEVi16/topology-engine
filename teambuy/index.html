The gap

Three people are stuck, and each one holds what another needs.

Operators

Can run it. Can’t fund it.

The skill is the part that takes ten years to acquire, and they already have it. What they do not have is $300,000 sitting in an account. The bank debt is available and cheap — the down payment is the wall.

Typical shortfall $240,000

Investors

Can fund it. Won’t run it.

Small-business equity returns are attractive and the day job is not. Capital that would happily write $400,000 against a profitable HVAC company will not move if the deal requires becoming an HVAC company.

How a team forms

Four steps, in this order. Step three is the one everyone skips.

  1. 1

    Profile and verify

    Operators document P&L history, headcount and sector. Investors show proof of funds and a real check range. Both sides are screened before either one appears on the board.

  2. 2

    Pair

    Matched on sector, check size, geography and — the one that quietly kills partnerships — how much control each side expects. Deal-breakers surface in the first conversation instead of the last.

  3. 3

    Sign the partnership terms

    Equity split, operator salary, distribution policy, buy-sell, and what happens when someone wants out. Agreed and signed before the team bids on anything. A handshake partnership falls apart at the first bad quarter; this is the part that makes a pair a buyer instead of two hopeful strangers.

  4. 4

    Go to market as one buyer

    The team approaches brokers with an operator named, funding evidenced and internal terms settled — the three things that usually take a broker four months to establish about a buyer.

A closed deal, start to finish

What the eleven months actually look like.

An illustrative walkthrough of the deal shape TeamBuy is built to produce — a $3.2M commercial HVAC company in Phoenix, bought by a team that did not exist four months before the letter of intent.

  1. Month 0

    Two profiles, no team

    Marcus has fourteen years running HVAC service and $85,000. Priya has $600,000 and no intention of learning the trade. Neither is a buyer.

  2. Month 2

    Paired and papered

    Sector, geography and control expectations line up. Partnership terms signed: Marcus takes 55% and a $140,000 salary, Priya takes 45% and quarterly reporting, with a five-year buy-sell at a set multiple.

  3. Month 4

    Letter of intent

    A broker sends a listing that fits. The team responds in nine days with a named operator, proof of funds and pre-qualification — not a question about the asking price.

  4. Month 7

    Financing committed

    $2.52M SBA 7(a) against a $685,000 injection — 21% down, double the floor, which is why the bank moved quickly. Diligence turns up customer concentration; the price comes down $110,000 to $3.2M rather than the deal dying.

  5. Month 11

    Closed

    Marcus runs it Monday morning. Priya gets a quarterly report. The broker keeps the full commission and TeamBuy takes 1% from the buyer side.

The hard questions

What people ask before they trust this.

Why would an operator give up equity instead of taking an SBA loan alone?

Because the loan is not the obstacle. SBA 7(a) requires a 10% equity injection — $300,000 on a $3M business — and most capable operators have a fraction of that. A silent partner closes the injection gap, not the whole capital stack. If you can fund the down payment yourself, you do not need us.

What stops the two partners from cutting TeamBuy out after the introduction?

Nothing, if all we did was make an introduction. The partnership agreement is executed through the platform and the success fee is written into it before either side sees a listing. A pair that wants to route around us can — they will just be doing their own papering, which is the part they came here for.

Is a 15% close rate really a buyer problem?

Only partly, and it is worth being honest about it. Most listings fail because they are overpriced, the books are messy, or the business cannot survive the owner leaving. Better buyers do not fix any of that. What a buyer team fixes is the subset of good businesses that lose their buyer at financing — which is where we think the recoverable deals are.

How is this different from a search fund?

A search fund raises capital first and then hunts for a business, usually above $5M, and usually run by someone with an MBA rather than a trade. We pair capital to an operator who already knows the industry, in the $1M–$6M band that search funds skip.

Are you licensed to take a fee on a business sale?

A transaction-based fee for introducing buyers to sellers can constitute brokering, and several states license it. Anywhere we operate we either hold the required license or structure the fee so it does not need one — confirmed with counsel per state before we take a dollar there.
