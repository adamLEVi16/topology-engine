The gap

Three people are stuck, and each one is holding what another needs.

Operators

Can run it. Can’t fund it.

A service manager who has run a $6M P&L for a decade still needs roughly $300,000 in cash to put 10% down on a $3M business. Most have a fraction of it. The skill is the hard part to acquire, and they already have it.

Investors

Can fund it. Won’t run it.

Small-business equity returns are attractive and the day job is not. Capital that would happily write $400,000 against a profitable HVAC company will not move if the deal requires showing up to dispatch trucks at 6am.

Brokers

Plenty of buyers. Few who close.

A listing draws dozens of inquiries and a handful of people who can actually fund it and run it. Sorting that is unpaid work. A buyer team arrives already sorted: named operator, verified funds, terms signed between them.

How a team forms

Four steps, in this order. Step three is the one everyone skips.

  1. 1

    Profile and verify

    Operators document P&L history, headcount and sector. Investors show proof of funds and a real check range. Both sides are screened before either one appears on the board.

  2. 2

    Pair

    Matched on sector, check size, geography and — the one that quietly kills partnerships — how much control each side expects. Deal-breakers surface in the first conversation instead of the last.

  3. 3

    Sign the partnership terms

    Equity split, operator salary, distribution policy, buy-sell, and what happens when someone wants out. Agreed and signed before the team bids on anything. A handshake partnership falls apart at the first bad quarter; this is the part that makes the pair a buyer instead of two hopeful strangers.

  4. 4

    Go to market as one buyer

    The team approaches brokers with an operator named, funding evidenced and internal terms already settled — the three things that usually take a broker four months to establish about a buyer.
